# Save the Cat! coaching guide

An independent paraphrase of public material. The full book was not available; this doesn't reproduce it or claim to have read all of it, and makes no claims from specific book pages.

Labels: **[G]** a principle seen through Phil Gyford's reading notes on Snyder, **[O]** later commentary on the official site, **[P]** an optional adaptation for games. Sources in §6. The questions below are material to choose from; ask one at a time.

## 1. Logline

### First, tell three kinds apart [P]

| Kind | What it conveys | Question to pick when stuck |
|---|---|---|
| Game marketing logline | what the player does and what experience to expect | After reading this line, what play should someone expect? |
| Dramatic campaign logline | who wants what, what stands in the way, what's at stake | What is lost if they fail? |
| Character premise | which choice this person faces in which situation | Which choice in this situation is hardest for this person in particular? |

Leave the approved game logline where it is. A new character premise is no reason to rewrite the marketing line around a character. If asked to change it, compare the expected experience of the current wording and the alternative; never swap it automatically.

### Snyder's checks [G: notes §1, §3]

| Principle | Check | Question |
|---|---|---|
| Irony, hook | An unexpected pairing makes you want to know what happens | Which two elements meet and break expectations? |
| Compelling mental picture | You can picture what happens as soon as you hear it | What one situation should the listener picture? |
| Audience and cost | You can guess who it's for and roughly how big it is | Who most wants to see this story? |
| Title | Fits the content and sticks with the logline | Read with the title, is the expected story clearer? |

Audience, cost and title are Snyder's film principles. [P] For games, check intended players and production scope separately and only when needed. Don't cram cost or title into the sentence, and there's no duty to invent a title. Film budget and game price or development cost are not the same thing.

[G: §3] When working on the hero, you can revisit the logline with an adjective for the hero, an adjective for the antagonist, and a goal people relate to. [O: The Logline] Current official commentary stresses hero, obstacle, goal and stakes. These are different layers of explanation; don't present them as Snyder's original four principles.

[P] If a dramatic logline is blurry, ask one thing: "What is the biggest obstacle to the goal?" With no single villain, check environment, institution or dilemma as the obstacle. Don't force this shape on marketing copy.

## 2. Hero

[G: §3, §6, §7] Snyder favours a hero who clashes hard with the situation, makes a big emotional journey, has a primal goal an audience gets at once, does something we can root for, and actively pursues the goal. Even an ensemble has a centre. [O: Introduction] Official commentary also describes the moment at first meeting when the audience starts to care.

| Where it's stuck | Question to pick |
|---|---|
| Centre unclear | Who takes the outcome of this story most to heart? |
| Goal abstract | What action or result would show they succeeded? |
| Goal doesn't land | What in this person's life is at risk if they don't get it? |
| Character apart from situation | Which trait of theirs makes this situation harder? |
| No grounds for sympathy | What action at first meeting makes you want to know how they end up? |
| Only reacting | Which of their own choices sets off the next event? |
| Change unclear | What can they choose at the end that they couldn't at the start? |

[P] A primal goal is a test of whether the need lands without explanation: survival, safety, belonging. It doesn't require a family tragedy. Sympathy isn't a mechanical good deed either; confirm which ground the writer chose (action, judgement or vulnerability). Don't force the player, the controlled character and the narrative centre into one person.

The ghost, lie, want and need chain in `craft.md` is K.M. Weiland's separate method. Don't credit it to Snyder. This guide doesn't use it as a required check.

## 3. Scene cards

[G: §5 / O: The Board] Put scenes on a board and move them around to test structure. On a card, `+/-` marks the emotional change and `><` the clash between what someone wants and what blocks it. The film board's fixed card count doesn't apply here.

[P] Sum up only the scenes you need, briefly. Leave fields the writer hasn't filled blank or "open".

```text
Scene: place and time / what happens here
Character +/-: whose state changes from start to end, and how
Player +/-: the player's state when the scene ends (may differ from the character's)
><: what's wanted / what blocks it / result left at the end
Play: what the player does or watches / effect on the next choice
```

| Diagnosis | Question to pick |
|---|---|
| Events but no visible change | How do their expectations differ at the end from the start? |
| Want not visible | What is this person trying to get in this scene? |
| Obstacle not visible | Why doesn't it go their way straight away? |
| Similar scenes repeat | If you cut this scene, which later choice stops making sense? |
| Detached from the game | What does the player do here themselves? |
| No change on the player's side | What does the player feel when this ends? The same as the character? |
| Character and player diverge | Is the divergence intended, or should they line up? |

### The player's side [P]

Snyder's `+/-` is the change in the character on screen. In a game the player lives through the scene, so note the player's change separately. They can diverge: the character grieves a lost ally while the player feels relief at barely surviving; the character thinks they've won while the player is uneasy because of an earlier clue.

A divergence may be a flaw or the point. The coach doesn't decide; ask the writer. The player field is also where you compare the scene against the project's core emotions, without narrowing it to one emotion word.

[P] Both marks are diagnostic. `+/-` doesn't limit a scene to light versus dark or to one emotion; mixed feelings still have a before and after. `><` doesn't demand a fight or argument: hiding, avoidance, environmental limits and diverging aims are obstacles too. If a rest, mood or information scene changes little, first check its job, and don't add forced conflict. Applying the film rule that every scene must turn is a choice in games, not a law.

## 4. Genre and the 15 beats

For the whole story's structure: set the scope (campaign or scenario), pick that scope's genre, then fill the 15 beats. It's not a one-pass questionnaire; you return to earlier beats as you write (§4.4). When checking only a logline, hero or single scene, don't ask for genre first.

### 4.1 Pick one genre

[G: §2] Snyder sorts every film into ten genres and says to study how films of the same genre work. [O: Genre Logline Templates, Get Started] Official commentary names three required elements per genre and explains that genres are sorted by the kind of change the hero goes through, not by setting or period. The summary below paraphrases both.

| # | Genre | Three elements [O] | Where it puts its weight [G] |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Monster in the House | monster, house, sin | someone's wrong summoned the monster; hide and flee in a closed space |
| 2 | Golden Fleece | road, team, prize | setting out after something; what the hero learns matters more than the events on the road |
| 3 | Out of the Bottle | wish, spell, lesson | magic grants a wish or punishes, but in the end magic isn't everything |
| 4 | Dude with a Problem | innocent hero, sudden event, life or death | an ordinary person in a situation too big; the stronger the villain, the better |
| 5 | Rites of Passage | life problem, wrong way, acceptance | loss, age, separation; only living through it solves it |
| 6 | Buddy Love | incomplete hero, counterpart, complication | two who push each other away learn they need each other; they split at the bottom and rejoin |
| 7 | Whydunit | detective, secret, dark turn | why more than who; the truth shows the darkness in people |
| 8 | Fool Triumphant | fool, establishment, transmutation | the underestimated one beats a bigger opponent |
| 9 | Institutionalized | group, choice, sacrifice | the gains and costs of putting the group before the self, often seen through a newcomer |
| 10 | Superhero | special power, nemesis, curse | an extraordinary person in an ordinary world; the cost of power and how they win sympathy |

[P] How to pick:

- One per scope. Campaign and scenario may differ.
- The coach doesn't choose. Offer the 2 or 3 genres closest to what the writer already said, plus "write my own" and "don't know yet".
- After the pick, ask about the one missing element of the three. For Monster in the House: "Whose wrong summoned the monster?" That answer becomes where the beats push.
- Story genre is a different axis from mood (horror) and game genre (deckbuilder, auto-battler). Being scary doesn't make it Monster in the House.
- Not picking doesn't block beat work. If the genre changes while writing, revisit its three elements first (§4.4).

### 4.2 Two layers: campaign and scenario [P]

Put one set of 15 beats on the whole campaign. Plan each scenario lightly, like one episode of a series; bring out its own 15 beats only to find a missing job. The default order is campaign beats first, then down to scenarios, but writing a scenario may change a campaign beat.

- One campaign beat may hold one scenario, several, or one scenario may span several beats. Never split the campaign's 15 beats into 15 scenarios. How many scenarios there are is a design decision, not a story-structure one.
- A scenario closes on its own from opening to final image, and you also decide which of its beats does the job of the campaign beat it sits in. Usually its finale and final image complete that job.
- A scenario's final image leads into the next scenario's opening state. Check that the campaign moved one step in between.
- The layers may point opposite ways. If a scenario ends in victory but sits in the campaign's All Is Lost slot, plant the campaign's loss inside the surface win. Look at it alongside the character and player divergence (§3).
- Name positions in both layers: `campaign · Bad Guys Close In 10/15 › scenario 2 · Catalyst 4/15`.

| Where it's stuck | Question to pick |
|---|---|
| Scenario's place unclear | Which campaign beat does this scenario sit in? |
| Campaign doesn't move | When this scenario ends, what changes in the campaign? |
| Themes apart | Is the question this scenario tests a small version of the campaign theme, or a different question? |
| Direction confused | Do the scenario ending and the campaign beat go the same way (up or down), or opposite? |

### 4.3 The 15 beats

[G: §4 / O: The Blake Snyder Beat Sheet] Each beat's job, paraphrased; the right column is a [P] coaching question. Decide which layer you're looking at, then choose only gaps or weak links.

| # | Beat | Job | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opening Image | shows the hero and world before change, and the story's tone | What state do we see at the start? |
| 2 | Theme Stated | plants the idea or question later choices will test | What question do you want to test to the end? |
| 3 | Set-Up | ordinary life, relationships, and what will become a problem | What is already wrong before anything happens? |
| 4 | Catalyst | something shakes ordinary life and demands a response | What makes it impossible to go on as before? |
| 5 | Debate | hesitation, refusal or preparation shows the weight of entering | What risk must they weigh before stepping in? |
| 6 | Break into Two | a choice moves them into a different situation | Which choice changes the way back? |
| 7 | B Story | a secondary relationship takes the theme from another angle | Which relationship handles the same question from a different angle? |
| 8 | Fun and Games | delivers the experience the premise promised | What experience does choosing this story promise? |
| 9 | Midpoint | a seeming win or loss shifts stakes and pressure | What new fact flips the meaning of what they achieved? |
| 10 | Bad Guys Close In | outside threats and inside cracks narrow the options | What makes the old way of coping stop working? |
| 11 | All Is Lost | the bottom, where the old way feels finished | What can they no longer rely on? |
| 12 | Dark Night of the Soul | accepting the loss, rethinking old beliefs | What do they rethink after failing? |
| 13 | Break into Three | links new understanding to a solution and acts again | What realisation makes them choose another way? |
| 14 | Finale | tests what was learned in action and resolves the central problem | How does the last choice prove they've changed? |
| 15 | Final Image | leaves a state of hero and world that contrasts with the start | What from the start can you show again to make the change visible? |

### Moving from film to games [P]

- The numbers are a map of jobs. Don't map film pages or percentages onto stages, rounds or play time. One beat can be several scenes; one scene can do several jobs.
- Don't lock B Story to romance or a joining ally. Don't force Theme Stated into exposition. Fun and Games doesn't require comedy; All Is Lost doesn't require a real death.
- Keep apart the official film commentary's change-and-triumph ending and a tragic or open ending the project may choose. When using the latter, say it's an adaptation.
- If branching, retries or choice order might make a player miss a turn, first ask "Must every player see this change?" Don't produce an answer or prescribe a new system.
- Rises and falls can also be read separately for the character and the player. Where the character hits All Is Lost, the player may have just won the battle.

### 4.4 Going back to fix

[G: §3, §5, §7] Snyder's method doesn't finish in one pass. Once you know the hero, go back and fix the logline; cards on the board are placed and moved; to enlarge the change at the end, push the start further back. The board is often set from the hinge beats first (Break into Two, Midpoint, All Is Lost, Break into Three).

[P] In coaching:

- Don't ask 1 to 15 once and stop. The writer may start from the hinges or from beats they already know.
- When a later answer contradicts an earlier beat, point it out once and ask: "Fix the earlier beat, or this answer?" The coach doesn't fix the earlier beat.
- Pairs to read together: Opening and Final Image; Set-Up and Finale (is the habit shown in Set-Up overcome in the Finale?); Theme Stated and Finale; Midpoint and All Is Lost (opposite directions?). When one side changes, mention its pair in a line.
- With two layers, reopen campaign beats when writing a scenario shakes them, and when a campaign beat changes, flag the scenarios inside it.
- Replace a changed beat with the writer's new sentence and read it back. If later beats leaned on it, flag them in a line; the writer decides whether to change them.

## 5. Applying and submitting [P]

When moving story into play, start from "What does the player choose, and what changes as a result?" New rules and numbers are not this skill's output.

Only when asked to submit a proposal or draft, attach this per item. Don't turn everyday coaching questions into approval paperwork.

```text
Item: [unapproved suggestion]
Basis: [G/O source and section] / [what was adapted as P]
Expected difference: [what the reader or player experiences differently]
Verdict: OK / NO / hold
memo: [the writer's reasons or requested changes; blank if none]
```

The coach never fills in the verdict and never widens what an `OK` covers. Record confirmed answers in the writer's words and read them back. Keep unapproved suggestions apart from confirmed answers.
