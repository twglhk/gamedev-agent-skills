# Chapter notes: *Save the Cat!* (Blake Snyder, 2005)

Contents: 0 how to read · ch. 1 logline · ch. 2 ten genres · ch. 3 the hero · ch. 4 the 15 beats · ch. 5 the board · ch. 6 immutable laws · ch. 7 fixing it · ch. 8 selling · common misreadings · sources

## 0. How to read

- This file answers "what exactly does the book say about beat X". It wasn't written from the full text; it cross-checks public notes that quote the book and official material, and restates them.
- Labels: **[G]** book content (with the secondary source it was seen through), **[O]** later or official commentary such as savethecat.com; a different book is named. Game adaptation is not in this file (that's `guide.md`).
- Source keys: `bm` = bookmarker.dellsystem.me excerpts of the book (with page numbers), `gy` = Phil Gyford's reading notes, `mb` = Mike Battle's book notes, `kh` = Kim Hartman's summary PDF (quotes the book heavily), `SB3` = chapter 3 of the sequel *Save the Cat! Strikes Back* (2009, free PDF), `trans` = the official workshop transition chart PDF.
- Two kinds of page number. "p.80" is a **book page** (per bm). A number beside a beat, like "(30)", is the **approximate page in a 110-page screenplay**. Don't mix them.
- Unchecked items are marked (unverified). Quotes are short and in quotation marks only.

## Ch. 1. What Is It? (logline)

- You must be able to say what the film is about in one sentence: the logline, or one-line. [G: gy §1, mb]
- Four elements of a good logline [G: gy §1, mb]
  1. Irony ("Isn't it ironic?"): an unexpected or intriguing pairing. The hook.
  2. A compelling mental picture: you can see the story, down to roughly how long it spans (one night, a weekend).
  3. Audience and cost: you sense who it's for and what it would cost.
  4. A killer title that "says what it is" and sticks with the logline.
- Write the logline **first**, not last, and use it to measure all later work. [G: mb "slave to the logline"]
- Roughly: until the pitch grabs me, don't bother with the story. [G: mb quotes "Until you have your pitch, and it grabs me, don't bother with the story"]
- Pitch the logline and title to friends and strangers; if they look bored, fix it and try again. [G: gy §1, mb]
- Adding primal roots (family, survival, mating) strengthens a logline. [G: mb "primal roots"] (Whether this is in ch. 1 or ch. 3 is unverified; kh puts "Primal urge" in ch. 3.)

## Ch. 2. Give Me the Same Thing... Only Different! (ten genres)

- Knowing your genre lets you learn how films of that genre work. The title comes from studios asking for "the same thing, only different". [G: gy §2, mb]
- You need to know hundreds of films well enough "to explain how they work"; the last five years aren't enough. Trace the lineage. [G: bm p.22]
- These aren't the usual horror, action or romance labels but ten groups by how the story works. [G: mb] Later official commentary says they're sorted by "the kind of change the hero goes through". [O: savethecat.com/get-started]

| Genre | Core elements per the book | Book's example films | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monster in the House | a confined space (the house), the "sin" that summoned the monster, the monster; then run and hide | Jaws, The Exorcist, Panic Room, Alien | [G: gy, mb, kh] |
| Golden Fleece | set out for one thing, find yourself; the people and events on the road are the plot, and what's learned matters more than the events | Star Wars, Back to the Future, The Wizard of Oz, heist and road movies | [G: gy, mb] |
| Out of the Bottle | a wish comes true (magic). At first we root for the wish because the hero's lot is sad, but they learn magic isn't everything. A punishment variant exists; then the hero must seem redeemable | Freaky Friday, Liar Liar, Groundhog Day, Bruce Almighty, Flubber | [G: gy, mb] |
| Dude with a Problem | an ordinary person in an extraordinary situation; the worse the villain, the more heroic the hero | Die Hard, Titanic, Schindler's List, The Terminator | [G: gy, mb] |
| Rites of Passage | life passages: puberty, midlife crisis, breakup, grief, addiction. Everyone but the hero knows the problem; the only fix is to go through it, ending in "that's life" acceptance | Ordinary People, 10, Days of Wine and Roses | [G: gy, mb] |
| Buddy Love | they dislike each other, then learn in the adventure they need each other; they split near All Is Lost and realise again. Usually one changes more. Love stories go here | Dumb & Dumber, Rain Man, E.T., Notting Hill | [G: gy, mb] |
| Whydunit | above all we want to know "why". What the audience learns about human nature matters more than the hero's change; the detective is the audience's stand-in. "Are we that bad?" | Chinatown, JFK, The Insider, Citizen Kane | [G: gy, mb] |
| The Fool Triumphant | the fool everyone ignores beats a bigger, establishment opponent; often has an "accomplice" who can't believe he gets away with it | Forrest Gump, Amadeus, Being There, The Jerk, Chaplin | [G: gy, mb, kh] |
| Institutionalized | a group, institution or "family". Honours the group while exposing how you lose yourself in it; often through a newcomer. "Who's crazier, me or them?" | Animal House, M\*A\*S\*H, One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest, American Beauty, The Godfather | [G: gy, mb, kh] |
| Superhero | the reverse of Dude with a Problem: an extraordinary person in an ordinary world. The hard part is sympathy, won from being misunderstood and from the envy of lesser people | Superman, Batman, Dracula, Frankenstein, Gladiator, A Beautiful Mind | [G: gy, mb, kh] |

- The official "three elements per genre" templates (e.g. Monster in the House = monster, house, sin) are a later arrangement. Whether the book splits them into threes is unverified. [O: savethecat.com genre logline templates]

## Ch. 3. It's About a Guy Who... (the hero)

- Add to the logline: an adjective for the hero, an adjective for the villain, and a goal anyone can relate to. [G: gy §3]
- You can start with only an idea and no "who". The "who" is chosen to make the "what" work better. [G: gy §3, mb]
- A good hero has **the most conflict** in the situation, **the longest emotional journey**, and the widest demographic appeal. [G: gy §3] mb and kh record the third as "a primal goal everyone will root for". Possibly an edition or passage difference (unverified).
- Stakes must be high and primal: survival, hunger, sex, protecting loved ones, fear of death. So mother and daughter, father and son, or spouses beat strangers. [G: mb quote, kh]
- Rule of thumb for finding the hero: someone I can identify with, learn from, have a reason to follow, believe deserves to win, whose stakes are primal and ring true. [G: bm p.62, kh]
- Write for an archetype, not a particular actor: the rising young fool, the good girl tempted, the clever kid, the sex symbol, the handsome man, the wounded soldier on a last redemptive mission, the wise grandfather. [G: gy §3, kh]
- In a biopic whose subject isn't heroic, adjust the facts slightly or set up another character as the way in. [G: gy §3]
- An ensemble still has a hero: regardless of screen time, the most likeable one with the most conflict who goes furthest. [G: gy §3]
- Once you've found the hero, go back and fix the logline. [G: gy §3]

## Ch. 4. Let's Beat It Out! (the 15 beats)

Overall frame [G]
- The 15 beats are the "Blake Snyder Beat Sheet (BS2)". Numbers in parentheses are approximate pages in a 110-page screenplay. [G: gy §4, mb]
- Snyder sees the three acts as three worlds: thesis, antithesis, synthesis. Act one records the "before" world, with a calm-before-the-storm sense that staying put means death. Act two is the upside-down version of act one; act three fuses them into a new world. [G: bm p.76, mb finale quote]

### 1. Opening Image (1)

- The film's first impression: tone, mood, type and scope are all here. [G: mb quote, kh]
- Often shows the hero's "before". [G: gy §4]
- Must be the opposite of the Final Image, contrasting like plus and minus, recording the emotional upheaval the film holds. Snyder writes that actors sometimes read only the first and last ten pages to see whether there's change. [G: kh]
- Misreading: any cool opening will do. The book treats it as the "before" snapshot paired with the end.

### 2. Theme Stated (5)

- In about the first five minutes, someone, usually not the hero, asks the hero a question or makes a remark. That is the theme. [G: bm p.73, gy, kh]
- Not overt; a passing line in conversation. The hero doesn't get it then, but it matters later. [G: bm p.73]
- Book's sample lines: "Be careful what you wish for", "Pride goeth before a fall", "Family is more important than money". [G: bm p.73]
- A good screenplay is the writer's argument: is this life, this goal, worth it? The rest proves or disproves the statement. [G: bm p.73, kh]
- Snyder says he always puts it on page 5. [G: kh]
- Misreading: the hero declares the theme. The book says "usually someone else, usually to the hero".

### 3. Set-Up (1-10)

- The first ten pages, twelve at most, where you hook or lose the reader. [G: kh, mb]
- Introduces, or hints at, every A-story character. [G: kh, gy]
- Plants every character tic and behaviour to be paid off later, and shows why and how the hero must change to win. [G: kh, mb, gy]
- Plants what the hero lacks or must fix as "Six Things That Need Fixing", which go off later like time bombs, get reversed and fixed, and become running gags and callbacks. [G: kh] (gy and mb give the content without the name.)
- A full record of the "before" world: it seems it will stay this way, but a storm is coming. [G: bm p.76]
- Book's examples: Big's "you must be this tall to ride", Romancing the Stone's isolated writer, Legally Blonde's Elle. [G: mb]
- Misreading: Set-Up is an exposition dump. In the book it's where the flaws and lacks to be fixed are planted. Too long, and it breaks the Laying Pipe law (ch. 6).

### 4. Catalyst (12)

- The first moment when "something happens". Without it the reader gets bored and writes "No Plot" on the coverage. [G: mb quote]
- If Set-Up showed how the world is, this knocks it down: a telegram, a firing, catching a spouse cheating, news of three days left, a knock on the door. [G: kh, mb]
- The life-changing event often comes looking like bad news, but by the end it's what led the hero to happiness. [G: kh, gy]
- Book's example: Elle getting dumped in Legally Blonde. [G: kh] (mb's Lord of the Rings, Ex Machina and Whiplash are mb's own examples; Ex Machina postdates the book.)

### 5. Debate (12-25)

- The hero's last chance to say "this is crazy". Should I go? Dare I? [G: kh, gy]
- It must pose a question. [G: kh, gy]
- Book's example: in Legally Blonde, the dumped Elle hits on Harvard Law as the fix, and "but can she get in?" becomes the Debate question; the section is her answering it. [G: kh, mb]
- mb also cites Die Hard (whether it's the book's example or mb's is unverified).
- Misreading: a scene where the hero must hesitate, like a refusal of the call. The book's requirement is a question.

### 6. Break into Two (25)

- Page 25 is the first place Snyder opens in someone else's script: does something happen, and does the writer know something must? [G: kh]
- Leave the old world (thesis) for its upside-down version (antithesis). The two worlds are distinct, so the step across must be clear. [G: kh, gy]
- The hero must not be lured, tricked or drift into act two. They must decide; being proactive is what makes a hero. [G: kh, mb quote]
- Page 25 at the latest. [G: gy]
- Misreading: events can drag the hero into act two.

### 7. B Story (30)

Every job the book gives the B Story [G: bm p.80, kh]
1. **A breather.** A brief rest from the A story. In Legally Blonde Elle is dumped, goes to law school, and school is hard; "enough, let's take a break", and she steps slightly off theme and meets someone new. [bm p.80]
2. **Usually "the love story"**, though not necessarily romance. Elle and Paulette, the Boston nail technician, are the love story. [bm p.80, kh]
3. **Where the hero is cared for.** [bm p.80]
4. **Where the hero talks about what they're learning.** Elle talks here about her struggles at Harvard. [bm p.80]
5. **Where the strength to push into act three comes from**, the last push to final victory. [bm p.80]
6. **Very often entirely new characters**, people we didn't meet in the first ten pages or know existed. [bm p.80, kh, gy]
7. **An upside-down version of the act-one characters.** Act two is the antithesis, so B-story characters invert the act-one world. Paulette is Elle's UCLA sorority sisters in a "funhouse mirror", hence a successful "classic anti-thesis creature". [bm p.80]
8. **Where the theme is discussed openly.** [bm p.80] It carries the theme. [kh, gy]
9. **Gives a cutaway from the A story.** [bm p.80]
10. **A small booster rocket** that smooths the A story's blatant act breaks. [kh]
11. **Mandatory, starting on page 30.** "You must have one". [bm p.80]

Where A and B meet
- Break into Three: the solution is found thanks to B-story characters and the theme talk in the B story; A and B meet and intertwine. [G: gy §4 Break into Three] [O: trans "The A Story and B Story have come together. The people from the B Story can kick you into three."]
- A and B cross at the Midpoint: Snyder writes this in the sequel. The Midpoint raises stakes for A and B alike, so the heroes often kiss there. [SB3 ch. 3] [O: trans] Whether the 2005 book has the same line is unverified.
- The sequel frames A = what the hero wants (outer goal, plot), B = what they need (spiritual goal), calling B the "helper story". B can be a lover, a mentor, a sidekick or a group (Patches in Dodgeball; the helper crowds in Legally Blonde, Miss Congeniality, Gladiator). [SB3]

Common misreadings
- Reducing B Story to a romantic subplot. The book says "usually a love story" and adds at once that it needn't be romance.
- Dropping the breather, new characters, inversion and cutaway functions. All appear together on p.80.
- Thinking B characters must be planted in act one. The book says they're often brand new.
- Treating the A/B Midpoint crossing as a rule of the first book.

### 8. Fun and Games (30-55)

- "The promise of the premise": the heart of the poster, where most trailer moments come from. [G: kh, gy, mb]
- Less concerned with forward motion; stakes don't rise until the Midpoint. Step away from stakes and enjoy what the idea is. [G: kh, gy]
- Lighter than other sections; good for set pieces. [G: gy, mb]
- Sequel addition: the name "fun" confused people (is a slasher's killing spree fun? Gladiator's slave fights?). Not every section is fun, but Fun and Games is the pitch and the poster, e.g. Sandra Bullock in Miss Congeniality in gown and tiara with a gun strapped to her leg. [SB3]
- mb cites Bruce Almighty, Spider-Man and Die Hard as the book's examples (unverified).
- Misreading: just "the fun scenes". Its job is delivering the premise as promised.

### 9. Midpoint (55)

- Either an "up", where the hero seems to peak (false victory), or a "down", where the world seems to collapse (false defeat). Snyder says he found this by watching hundreds of films. [G: mb quote, kh, gy]
- Stakes rise here. [G: kh, mb]
- Fun and Games ends and the story "gets back to" the story. [G: kh, gy]
- Rule: the Midpoint is not as good as it seems and All Is Lost is not as bad, or the reverse. [G: kh]
- Book's example: Jim Carrey getting everything he wanted in Bruce Almighty, a false victory. [G: mb]
- Later commentary adds that a ticking clock starts here. [O: trans, savethecat.com/how-to-write-a-screenplay] Whether the first book says so is unverified.
- A/B crossing: see beat 7 (sequel source).

### 10. Bad Guys Close In (55-75)

- The bad guys regroup and send in heavy artillery. Inside, dissent, doubt and jealousy break up the hero's team. [G: kh, gy]
- Forces close in from outside and inside. Evil doesn't give up, and the hero has nowhere to turn; they're on their own. [G: mb quote, kh]
- If the Midpoint looked like a win, things start to go wrong here. [G: gy]
- The hardest section to write. [G: gy]
- Sequel addition: hard because inside, the hero clings to old beliefs that are shown false one by one. [SB3]
- Book's example films: unverified.

### 11. All Is Lost (75)

- The opposite of the Midpoint in its up or down. A false defeat that looks total. [G: gy, mb]
- Put in something to do with death; Snyder says it works every time, whether essential or symbolic: a potted flower, a goldfish, an aunt's obituary. [G: kh] The "whiff of death". [G: mb]
- Where the mentor goes to die. [G: mb] The sequel says "all mentors go to page 75 to die". [SB3]
- Book's example: Will Ferrell looking down from the bridge in Elf. [G: mb] (mb only; unverified.)
- Misreading: the death must be a real character's. Symbolic works.

### 12. Dark Night of the Soul (75-85)

- The hero's hopeless bottom, with no one and no idea to help. [G: gy]
- The darkness before dawn; may last five seconds or five minutes. [G: mb]
- Just before the hero digs deep for the last, best idea that will save them and everyone, though they can't see it yet. [G: mb quote]
- Sequel addition: Catalyst, Debate, Break into Two and All Is Lost, Dark Night, Break into Three have the same shape. Something is done to the hero, they hesitate, they respond actively; the second time the stakes are higher. Dark Night ties to a moment of clarity when the hero sees their own fault. [SB3]

### 13. Break into Three (85)

- The solution is found, thanks to B-story characters, the theme talk in the B story, and the A-story struggle to beat the bad guys. The two stories meet and intertwine. [G: gy §4]
- The act-two break; the hero gets back up and goes all the way. [G: mb]
- The B story can kick the hero into act three. [O: trans]
- Misreading: luck or outside rescue gets them into act three. In the book's structure, what's gained from B and the theme becomes the solution.

### 14. Finale (85-110)

- The wrap-up: lessons applied, tics overcome, A and B ending in the hero's triumph, the old world turned over into a new order, all led by the hero from what they lived through in the upside-down world of act two. [G: mb quote, gy]
- Dispatch the bad guys from the bottom up: henchmen first, boss last. The source of the problem (person or thing) must be fully dealt with for the new order to stand. [G: mb quote, gy]
- The first book's account is short. Snyder admitted being called out for writing only "Finale" and added the "Five-Point Finale" in the sequel. [SB3] Those five steps are the sequel's; don't present them as the first book's.

### 15. Final Image (110)

- The opposite of the Opening Image: proof that change happened and is real. [G: mb quote, gy]
- If you lack such a final image or can't see how it fits, go back and redo the math; something in act two doesn't fit. [G: mb quote]

## Ch. 5. Building the Perfect Beast (the board)

- A corkboard or wall of index cards, "the board", in four rows: act one (1-25), act two first half (25-55), act two second half (55-85), act three (85-110). [G: gy §5, mb]
- One card = one scene, about 40 cards in all. mb's quote: "40 scenes. No more". Nine or ten per row. [G: gy, mb]
- Pin scenes you already have, then fix the hinge beats: Break into Two at the end of row one, Midpoint at the end of row two, its reversal All Is Lost, Break into Three at the end of row three. [G: gy]
- A chase or montage that feels like several cards still folds into one. [G: gy]
- Colour-code by storyline (each character's story, theme points, C, D, E stories). [G: gy, mb]
- `+/-`: the emotional change within a scene. Every scene is a small movie with beginning, middle and end, and must have an emotional change; if not, you don't know what the scene is about. All pluses means the hero faces no trials. [G: gy, mb quote]
- `><`: conflict. Each character in a scene wants something and has an obstacle; who wins? One conflict per scene; if you can't find it, make one. [G: gy, mb quote]
- The board shows gaps and clumps. [G: mb]

## Ch. 6. The Immutable Laws of Screenplay Physics

- **Save the Cat:** at first meeting the hero *does* something that makes us like them and want them to win. If not nice, at least likeable, and make the villain worse. Examples: Al Pacino in Sea of Love; Vincent in Pulp Fiction, set against a scarier boss. [G: gy §6, mb]
- **The Pope in the Pool:** bury exposition in an eye-catching, surprising or funny scene. Explain the plot while the Pope swims and the audience is busy with the pool. [G: gy, mb]
- **Double Mumbo Jumbo:** one piece of magic per movie. Spider-Man bitten by a spider plus the Green Goblin from a chemical accident is too much. [G: gy, mb]
- **Laying Pipe:** don't lay too much before the story starts. Minority Report takes 40 minutes to the hook; Along Came Polly starts the dating only after a first wedding, honeymoon and breakup. [G: gy, mb]
- **Black Vet (Too Much Marzipan):** don't pile things on one idea. Named after Mel Brooks's joke about a veteran who is also a veterinarian. [G: gy, mb]
- **Watch Out for That Glacier!:** danger must be here and now; slowly approaching danger isn't scary. Examples: Dante's Peak, Outbreak. [G: gy]
- **The Covenant of the Arc:** every character except the villain changes during the film. Stories are about change; good people embrace it, bad people refuse it. [G: bm p.136, gy, mb]
- **Keep the Press Out!:** no reporters. E.T. has no journalists covering the alien, so the story stays in the family, the neighbourhood, us versus them. Exception: when the press or a world event is the subject. A lesson Snyder credits to Spielberg. [G: gy, mb]

## Ch. 7. What's Wrong with This Picture? (fixing it)

- When done, let it sit at least a week, then reread. [G: gy §7]
- **The Hero Leads:** the hero must not be dragged along. Checks: is the goal stated clearly in the Set-Up; do clues come to the hero or does the hero go to them; active or passive; do others give orders or does the hero? [G: gy]
- **Talking the Plot:** don't have characters explain back story. Show, don't tell. To show two people fought, have them talk about anything but the fight. [G: bm p.147, gy]
- **Make the Bad Guy Badder:** hero and villain are a pair, equal in skill and power, and the villain is ready to do anything. He must seem unbeatable. [G: gy]
- **Turn, Turn, Turn:** the plot must not just move forward but get faster, more complex and more intense, exposing the heroes' flaws, betrayals, doubts and fears and the threats they face. [G: gy]
- **The Emotional Color Wheel:** use every emotion; the audience should live the film like a good dream. [G: bm p.152, gy]
- **"Hi How Are You I'm Fine":** every line should show the speaker's past or world view. Cover the names and you should still know who's talking. [G: gy]
- **Take a Step Back:** to show change, push the starting point further back. If they're bright at the end, they must start that far away. [G: gy]
- **A Limp and an Eyepatch:** one memorable visual mark, even for bit parts. [G: gy]
- **Is It Primal?:** every character's goal must have a primal root: protect the family (Die Hard), defend the home (Home Alone), find a mate (Sleepless in Seattle), revenge (Gladiator), survival (Titanic). Even a stockbroker's story survives if you give him a primal urge. [G: bm p.159, gy, kh]

## Ch. 8. Final Fade In (selling)

- The last chapter is about selling the script. Its title and boundaries are unverified in these notes; the points below are mb's account of the book.
- Being talented and writing the perfect script is a small part of what you need. Get out of the room and meet people. [G: mb quote]
- Make a plan: list what you have (scripts, pitches) and what you need (agent, producer). [G: mb]
- First contact, by letter or anything else, is steady self-promotion. Think long-term reputation, don't burn bridges, ask what the other side wants and how you can help. [G: mb quote]
- An agent is easier to get with a deal on the table. If a legitimate company offers an option, even for little money, take it. [G: mb quote]
- Do: festivals, classes, writers' groups, building a name as a reviewer, moving to LA to work as a script reader. Don't: screenplay contests (a "colossal waste of time" in his view), overdone publicity stunts. [G: mb]
- Two of Snyder's own agent stories (a flyer; a chance meeting at a bar). [G: mb]
- As mb notes, this is the industry as of 2005, and much has changed. [O: mb's comment]

## Common misreadings at a glance

| Beat or item | Common misreading | What the book says | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Theme Stated | the hero states the theme | usually another character, usually to the hero, in passing | bm p.73 |
| Set-Up | exposition section | plants tics and lacks to fix (Six Things), introduces every A character | kh |
| Debate | a hesitation scene | must pose one question | kh, gy |
| Break into Two | swept in by events | the hero decides; no luring, tricking or drifting | kh, mb |
