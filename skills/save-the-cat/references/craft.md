# Story craft for coaching

Contents: Part 1 story frames (A kishōtenketsu · B three acts · C hero's journey · D story circle · E seven-point structure · F investigative cosmic horror · G the assemble; Save the Cat's 15 beats are in `guide.md` §4.3) · Part 2 mixing frames · Part 3 how Arkham Horror: The Card Game builds a campaign · Part 4 story to play · Part 5 how to ask · Part 6 choosing the protagonist · Sources

This is a reference for coaching, not a place for game rules or decisions. Each frame lists its beats, with what each beat *does* and a question *to ask*. Adapt the questions to the writer's game.

## Part 1. Story frames

### A. Kishōtenketsu: four

The East Asian four-part structure (from classical Chinese quatrains; *kishōtenketsu* in Japanese). Unlike the Western three acts, **conflict is not the backbone.** The third part, *ten*, is a twist that makes you see what came before differently.

| # | Beat | Does | Ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ki (introduction) | shows the characters and place | When the story opens, where are they and what are they doing? |
| 2 | Shō (development) | continues without major change | How does the place gradually reveal itself? What do they learn? |
| 3 | Ten (twist) | a sideways turn; earlier events read differently | What revelation makes everything so far read differently? |
| 4 | Ketsu (conclusion) | ties the twist to what came before | Faced with what they now know, what do they do? |

- Fits: investigation and mystery, where revelation matters more than fighting. Good for gathering a drop from light into dread at the single *ten* point.

### B. Three acts: three

The most common film structure: set-up, confrontation, resolution, with **turning points** handing off between acts.

| # | Beat | Does | Ask |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Act 1 set-up | ordinary life and the inciting event | What pulls the heroes into this? |
| 1→2 | Turning point 1 | a decision with no way back | When can they no longer back out? |
| 2 | Act 2 confrontation | pursuing the goal as obstacles grow | What blocks them more and more? How does it flip around the middle? |
| 2→3 | Turning point 2 | the lowest point, the last key | The moment all seems lost, and the last clue they grab? |
| 3 | Act 3 resolution | the climax and after | What's at stake in the final fight, and what changes after? |

- Fits: a single mission with a clear goal, a stage that ends with a boss.

### C. The hero's journey: twelve (Vogler)

Joseph Campbell's *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949), condensed for screenwriting by Christopher Vogler (*The Writer's Journey*). Example: *Captain America: The First Avenger* (2011).

| # | Beat | Does | Ask | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ordinary world | the hero before leaving | Who is this hero before the adventure, and what do they lack? | frail Steve rejected by the army |
| 2 | Call to adventure | an event or person offers the task | What calls the hero? | Dr Erskine's experiment |
| 3 | Refusal of the call | hesitation, fear | Why don't they step up at once? (skip if none) | none |
| 4 | Meeting the mentor | someone gives wisdom or tools | Who hands them what? | Erskine: "stay a good man" |
| 5 | Crossing the threshold | entering the other world | The moment they cross a line with no return? | the serum |
| 6 | Tests, allies, enemies | learning the rules, sides form | Whom do they join, and whom do they clash with? | rescuing Bucky, the Howling Commandos |
| 7 | Approach to the inmost cave | preparing for the great ordeal | What do they prepare before the climax? | planning the Hydra base assault |
| 8 | Ordeal | closest to death or failure | The worst moment? Whom do they lose? | Bucky falls from the train |
| 9 | Reward | what survival earns | What do they gain in return? | Red Skull's location |
| 10 | The road back | heading back, pursued | What's left to finish? | boarding the bomber |
| 11 | Resurrection | the final test using all they learned | Which choice shows the hero has truly changed? | crashing the plane into the ice |
| 12 | Return with the elixir | what they bring to the world | What remains for the world or the team? | waking 70 years later |

- Fits: **the whole campaign's skeleton**, one hero's growth. All twelve inside one scenario is heavy.

### D. Story circle: eight (Dan Harmon)

Dan Harmon (*Community*, *Rick and Morty*) cut the hero's journey down to one TV episode. The core: **every gain is followed by a price.**

| # | Beat | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | You: a zone of comfort | What state is the hero in as this starts? |
| 2 | Need | What do they want? What do they lack? |
| 3 | Go | What door leads into the unfamiliar? |
| 4 | Search | What goes wrong there, and what do they learn? |
| 5 | Find | Do they get what they wanted? Is it what they thought? |
| 6 | Take | What price do they pay? |
| 7 | Return | How do they get back to the familiar? |
| 8 | Change | What's different now? |

- Fits: a single episode, one that turns into black comedy or tragedy. Chains well into campaigns of many linked scenarios.

### E. Seven-point structure (Dan Wells)

From horror author Dan Wells on *Writing Excuses*. **Decide the ending first, set the start as its opposite, then fill the middle.**

| # | Beat | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | Resolution (write first) | What state are the heroes in when this ends? |
| 1 | Hook (opposite of the resolution) | What's the opposite starting state? |
| 4 | Midpoint | When do they turn from reacting to acting? |
| 2 | Plot turn 1 | What event pushes the hero into the story? |
| 3 | Pinch 1 | When does the enemy first show its power? |
| 5 | Pinch 2 | When does it tighten harder? What do they lose? |
| 6 | Plot turn 2 | What's the last piece needed to win? |

- Fits: **a doom clock closing in.** Pinches 1 and 2 line up with doom events.

### F. Investigative cosmic horror

Conventions settled in Lovecraft's fiction and the *Call of Cthulhu* tabletop RPG (not one person's formula). **Knowing is the danger.**

| # | Beat | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ordinary start | What first scene looks like nothing's wrong? |
| 2 | First anomaly | What first trace doesn't add up? |
| 3 | Investigation | Where are the clues scattered? |
| 4 | Growth | Joined up, what do the clues reveal that's far bigger than expected? |
| 5 | Revelation | What truth can't be unseen? |
| 6 | Confrontation | Fight, seal, or flee? |
| 7 | Scars | What stays with the survivors? |
| 8 | Open end | Is it really over? What remains? |

- Fits: Arkham Horror-style scenarios. If your heroes are powerful rather than helpless, beat 6 leans towards a fight they can win.

### G. The assemble: a team comes together

The common flow of team-up stories like *The Avengers* and *Ocean's Eleven* (no single source).

| # | Beat | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A job no one can do alone | Why won't one person do? |
| 2 | The recruiter | Who gathers the team? |
| 3 | Joining | Why does each new member come? What do they want? |
| 4 | Friction | Where do they clash? |
| 5 | Roles | What does each one handle? |
| 6 | It goes wrong | Where does the plan collapse? |
| 7 | As one | The moment they trust each other and click? |
| 8 | After | Does the team stay? Who leaves? |

- Fits: **scenarios where an ally joins.** It maps directly onto two linked scenarios, before and after the join.

## Part 2. Mixing frames

You don't have to build everything with one frame. Use a different one per layer.

| Combination | How | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Outer journey + inner three acts** | hero's journey (C) over the campaign, three acts (B) per scenario | setting the campaign skeleton first |
| **Outer journey + inner kishōtenketsu** | campaign is C; each scenario's *ten* peels one layer off the campaign's secret | investigation and mystery build per scenario |
| **A/B plot** | A = outer events (doom clock and goal), B = the hero's inner story (fear, relationships). In the climax, B's change solves A | carrying a character story inside one scenario |
| **A circle per scenario** | each scenario closes with Harmon's circle (D) through the price; the campaign flows with Save the Cat (`guide.md` §4.3) | wanting each episode to end with a black-comedy snap |
| **Two scenarios = one assemble** | first scenario covers assemble (G) 1 to 3, the second 4 to 8; together one team-up story | an ally's joining spans two scenarios |

- There are two layers, **campaign and scenario.** Ask first which layer the writer is working on.

## Part 3. How Arkham Horror: The Card Game builds a campaign

- **Parts of one scenario:** the act deck (the heroes' goal, usually advanced by collecting clues), the agenda deck (the enemy's plan as a clock; it advances as doom piles up and the scenario gets worse), locations (shroud value and clues), the encounter deck (enemies and events), resolutions (several branches) and the campaign log.
- **Resolutions branch.** Whether you won, ran out of time or were all defeated, you read a different resolution and write the result in the campaign log. The next scenario's setup changes based on that log.
- **Night of the Zealot (core set campaign, three scenarios):** in The Gathering, you break out of your own house past ghouls and choose whether to burn it. In The Midnight Masks, you chase cultists across the city before midnight (how many you catch is logged). The Devourer Below is the final fight to stop a ritual in the woods. Each earlier log changes the later setup.
- Lessons: (1) each scenario runs its own goal and clock; (2) earlier choices change later scenarios; (3) allies who join can depend on resolutions.

## Part 4. Story to play

Map each story element to the part of your game that carries it. Rename the right column for your own systems. The examples assume a campaign game with scenarios, locations and battles.

| In the story | In the game (example) | Ask when mapping |
|---|---|---|
| What the hero wants to achieve | goal stages (e.g. N clues → gatekeeper → boss) | How many stages, and what moves each one on? |
| The enemy's plan, time pressure | a doom clock and doom events | As time passes, what happens on the enemy's side, in order? |
| The stage | a location graph (clues, test targets and events per location) | How many locations, how linked? Where are clues hidden? |
| Obstacles beaten by skill or judgement | skill tests (stat + roll vs target) | Which stat beats it? What happens on failure? |
| Obstacles beaten by fighting | battles (enemy line-up, placement, resolution) | Which enemies, how many? |
| The antagonist | gatekeepers, bosses and their abilities | What moves does the antagonist use in a fight? |
| The hero's growth | reward items or upgrades | What could they earn here? |
| The price | injury, rising doom, losing an ally | What do they pay? |
| Twist (*ten*, Midpoint) | a goal stage advancing, a doom event | When is the twist revealed: on reaching a goal, or when time runs out? |
| A drop from light to dread | a turning point inside the scenario (staging, music, enemy swap) | At what moment does the mood drop? |
| Branching endings | resolutions plus a campaign log | How does it end on a win, on time out, on defeat? What gets logged? |
| How an ally joins | an end-of-scenario notice ("you found X; a new ally can be recruited") | Whom do they meet, and how do they join? |
| A new hero's identity | hero stats, role, abilities | What job does this hero do in a fight? |

### Fit check: what the story says and what play does

| If the story says | Play must also |
|---|---|
| "You can't do this alone" | make the scenario before the join actually hard |
| "There's no time" | make the doom clock actually squeeze choices |
| "Knowing is dangerous" | make collecting clues invite a price |
| "The tide turns" | make battles actually get easier as gear, allies and synergy come together |
| "A hero dies" | make that death change the next scenario's setup |

## Part 5. How to ask

### The shape of one beat question

1. **Where you are.** Name the frame and position, e.g. `now = kishōtenketsu, ten (3/4)`.
2. **What this beat does**, one line, with one example line if needed.
3. **One question.** You may attach 2 or 3 **prompt options** fitted to the writer's premise; the writer picks one or writes their own. Options are material to spark ideas, not answers.
4. Play the answer back in one line, **in the writer's words.**
5. Only if the answer is vague, ask **one follow-up** from the list below. At most once per beat.

### Follow-up questions

| Question | Use when |
|---|---|
| What does this hero want? | the character is only being dragged along |
| What stops them? | no obstacle is visible |
| What must they give up to get it? | victory feels cheap |
| Why is this happening now? | the trigger is weak |
| Why must it be this hero? | anyone could fill the role |
| What if the worst happens? | tension is low |
| What is the player doing with their hands at that moment? | the story drifts away from the game |
| What would the player never expect? | it's running predictably |
| What does this place or enemy say about the world? | the stage feels empty |
| What carries over to the next scenario when this one ends? | scenarios feel disconnected |
| Light side or dread side, where does this scene stand? | a tonal drop is blurry |

### What the coach doesn't do

- Record plot, names or setting the writer didn't choose as if decided.
- Ask about several beats at once.
- Polish the writer's sentences on its own. If something could be tightened, suggest it and let them choose.

## Part 6. Choosing the protagonist (guide and criteria)

Score candidates against these criteria before suggesting any, and show the weak cells.

### What the best do

- **Snyder:** fix the "what" first, then choose the "who" who would suffer that situation most. Most conflict, longest emotional journey, primal goal (survival, family, revenge). Then rewrite the logline as hero adjective + villain adjective + goal. Even ensemble stories have one protagonist.
- **K.M. Weiland:** a past wound (the ghost) leaves the protagonist one belief (the lie). What they chase on the surface (the want) differs from what they need inside (the need), and before the end they must give up the want to get the need. The further apart the first belief and the final realisation, the longer the arc.
- **Mass Effect 2:** a team-gathering game gains power when each mission carries a teammate's personal stakes (recruitment, loyalty, suicide missions). Putting the protagonist on a leash (the Illusive Man) makes the player fight their own character. What the protagonist wants and what the player wants to do should match.

### Criteria: rate each high, medium or low

| # | Criterion | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Conflict with the team | Are the people they must gather the ones who find them hardest to accept? |
| 2 | Irony against the antagonist | Does clashing with this particular opponent break the protagonist's goal? |
| 3 | Primal goal | Does it land without explanation (family, revenge, survival)? |
| 4 | Longest road | Are the first belief (the lie) and the final realisation opposites? Does the end reach the game's core emotion? |
| 5 | Fit with play | Is what the protagonist wants the reason for the game's core verbs? Does the player want the same thing as this person? |
| 6 | Pull across scenarios | Does it carry the whole campaign, not just one scenario? |

- If any cell is low, suggest a way to fill it alongside.
